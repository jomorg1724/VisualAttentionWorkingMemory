01 · Introduction

One place to attend.
Many things to see.

A location can contain an oriented edge, a moving object, a familiar photograph, or a memory of something that is no longer visible. Does selecting that location require a different attention mechanism for each kind of information, or can a common spatial priority signal govern them all?

From selective enhancement to selective use

Vision supplies more information than behavior requires. Selecting the relevant evidence is therefore a computational problem in its own right, distinct from representing the scene accurately. A photograph may be recognized without its location being behaviorally relevant; an accurately represented movement may still produce the wrong answer if it belongs to a distractor. Classical accounts of selective attention describe competition between representations and the influence of behavioral relevance on that competition. Here, “attention” means a mechanism that changes which spatially organized evidence influences memory and report. [[desimone1995]] [[moran1985]] [[reynolds1999]]

Experiments linking frontal eye fields and superior colliculus to covert selection motivate a distinction between the fidelity of sensory representations and the use made of them. Subthreshold stimulation of frontal eye fields can modulate spatially corresponding visual responses; stimulation of superior colliculus can improve spatially specific perceptual performance without an overt eye movement. Conversely, collicular inactivation can impair selection even when conventional cortical signatures of attention remain. These observations make selective routing an experimentally meaningful hypothesis. [[moore2003]] [[muller2005]] [[cavanaugh2004]] [[zenon2012]]

The superior colliculus combines layered sensory and motor organization with distributed interactions. We study one computational abstraction of this organization: the spatial priority map: activity indexed by position that reflects the relevance of competing information. The hypothesis examined here is computational: the same location-dependent weights can select distinct feature channels without separate motion-attention and appearance-attention controllers. [[krauzlis2013]] [[fecteau2006]] [[bisley2010]]

Why recurrence belongs in the question

Selection depends on history. A cue can disappear before the event it identifies; a probe can query an item after intervening images; a brief movement can contribute evidence that must survive a blank interval. Persistent neuronal activity, transient population dynamics, and latent synaptic mechanisms provide different accounts of how relevant information can outlast its stimulus. Together they motivate testing an observer whose internal state evolves as the trial unfolds. [[fuster1971]] [[funahashi1989]] [[compte2000]] [[wang2001]] [[mongillo2008]] [[stokes2013]] [[rose2016]]

Morgan, Albanna, and Herman introduced a recurrent vision transformer in which spatial memory influences attention. Their reported orientation-change results included cue-dependent behavior, temporal attention dynamics, and perturbation effects. The published preprint used reward-based learning and an action-timing policy. This manuscript extends its memory-guided selection hypothesis to a broader task battery. Our current observer uses supervised terminal decisions, normalized GRUs, explicit local motion comparisons, and a single recurrent spatial pooling map. [[morgan2025]]

The central extension

Can a common recurrent selection mechanism coordinate heterogeneous sensory representations and different memory demands, while remaining causally manipulable at a shared spatial interface?

A common selector can coordinate distinct sensory codes

Parallel sensory pathways can supply a shared attention controller. The influential perception/action distinction organizes visual processing by the uses of visual information. Our model organizes evidence into appearance and temporal correspondence. Both routes can carry information useful for the same judgment, and both meet at a common spatial selector. [[goodale1992]]

Motion is a relation between images. Orientation and color are properties that can be estimated within an image, although comparing them across time still requires memory. Image-memory tasks require retaining the identity of successive photographs, while motion tasks require comparing movement across observations. We retain an RGB appearance pathway alongside a local temporal-correspondence pathway and combine them before spatial selection. Direction-selective population models motivate the latter; feature and conjunction judgments motivate preserving the former. [[adelson1985]] [[watson1985]] [[simoncelli1998]] [[treisman1980]] [[spatial-luck-1997]]

A motion population retains evidence for several possible displacements simultaneously. That distinction matters when two motions coexist, textures repeat, or dots disappear. Averages can erase these alternatives. The observer therefore has access to both a population distribution and a vector derived from it, and it compares local populations before spatial pooling. The engineered displacement bins and local comparisons specify an inductive bias about correspondence and temporal structure. Task labels and pixel-prediction errors train the learned components. [[ma2006]] [[pack2001]] [[rust2006]]

What would make the extension scientifically informative?

01

Common causal selection

Does a perturbation at one spatial site alter use of both static and moving evidence at that site, with the direction of the behavioral effect determined by whether it is target or distractor?

02

History-dependent relevance

Can the map preserve a precue, then redirect selection following a retrospective cue? Encoding, maintenance, and retrieval perturbations should be separated.

03

Comparison before pooling

Does maintaining per-location temporal evidence prevent a foil change from being confused with a target change? Matched local-comparison ablations would test necessity.

04

Robustness across demands

Do effects persist across delay, memory load, stimulus difficulty, and independent training seeds? Held-out tasks provide a separate test of transfer.

These questions connect task performance to the computations producing it. To measure how cues and selected evidence influence decisions, the experiments combine task performance with matched stimuli, phase-specific interventions, location controls, and analyses of both target reports and distractor errors. Biological work linking motion representations or collicular activity to choice illustrates the value of perturbations, while also demonstrating that sensory evidence and choice bias must be considered separately. [[newsome1988]] [[salzman1990]] [[herman2018]] [[sridharan2014]]

The evidence now available

The completed 13-task suite showed broad performance with remaining difficult conditions. A continuation added VDA4, VDA9, and VDA16 while preserving the shared architecture. In its frozen VDA4 experiment, inhibiting a perfectly cued bottom-right region increased detection of unexpected top-left changes from 48.2% to 68.0%, while reducing bottom-right detection from 87.0% to 6.5%. This is evidence for a location-dependent trade-off in one trained model. Earlier motion and cued-orientation tests yielded null results for their primary balanced-accuracy benefit endpoints.
