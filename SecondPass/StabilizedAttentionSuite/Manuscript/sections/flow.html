04 · A tutorial on motion

How can pixels tell us
that something moved?

Before there can be attention to motion, there must be evidence for motion. This section develops that evidence from first principles, then locates our deliberately small correspondence model among classical and learned optical-flow methods.

Images, displacement, and velocity are different objects

A pixel is one addressable cell in the image grid. Grayscale intensity is the single number assigned to that cell to encode its light–dark level. In the model's input convention, 0 encodes black, 1 encodes white, and 0.5 encodes the midpoint gray. A value such as 0.8 therefore specifies a lighter gray than 0.2. These are dimensionless image values: their scale is set by how the image is encoded. Physical light output from a screen additionally depends on its display calibration.

A color pixel contains three numbers, called channels, for its red, green, and blue components (RGB). The motion pathway constructs one grayscale number by taking their arithmetic mean: add the three channel values and divide by three. If a pixel has red 0.9, green 0.6, and blue 0.3, its grayscale intensity is (0.9 + 0.6 + 0.3)/3 = 0.6. The appearance pathway also receives the three separate color channels.

\[I(x,y,t)=\frac{X_R(x,y,t)+X_G(x,y,t)+X_B(x,y,t)}{3}.\]
Read the notation. \(I(x,y,t)\) means “look up the grayscale value at column \(x\), row \(y\), in frame \(t\).” The letter I names the grayscale image sequence; the parentheses specify which entry to read. \(X_R,X_G,X_B\) name the red, green, and blue channel arrays at that same location and frame. R, G, and B are channel labels. A scalar is one number, such as I = 0.6. A vector is an ordered collection of numbers, such as the RGB triple (0.9, 0.6, 0.3).

Each model frame has 100 columns and 100 rows. We index them from 0 to 99: \(x=0,y=0\) is the upper-left pixel, x increases to the right, and y increases downward. The frame index t counts successive observations. Thus \(I(20,30,5)=0.8\) says that the pixel at column 20, row 30 of frame 5 has grayscale value 0.8. The compact phrase “an image is a function of position” means that specifying a location returns its pixel value. A movie adds a frame index to this lookup.

Displacement describes a change in position. Suppose the same visible dot is centered at (20,30) in one observation and (24,28) in a later observation. Its displacement is \(\mathbf d=(d_x,d_y)=(24-20,28-30)=(4,-2)\) pixels: four pixels right and two pixels up. Bold d denotes the two-component vector; its entries \(d_x\) and \(d_y\) are the horizontal and vertical changes. “Corresponding image structure” means the dot, edge, or other pattern we are trying to match between those observations.

Velocity describes displacement per unit time. Write \(\Delta t\) for the elapsed interval between the observations; the symbol Δ means a difference. Then \(\mathbf v=\mathbf d/\Delta t=(d_x/\Delta t,d_y/\Delta t)\). If the example observations are two frame steps apart, \(\Delta t=2\) and \(\mathbf v=(2,-1)\) pixels per frame step. The implementation uses frame-step intervals of 1, 2, or 4 when forming its motion estimates. Converting these values to pixels per second requires a frame rate; converting them to degrees of visual angle per second also requires the angular size represented by each pixel.

Optical flow describes apparent movement of image structure. Physical object motion, retinal projection, and measured optical flow describe different stages of image formation and estimation. Shadows, specularities, occlusion, changing contrast, and replacement of dots can all alter brightness without transporting one enduring object. A moving grating also admits several apparent correspondences. The model's motion features describe image-based correspondence evidence. [[barron1994]]

Start with brightness constancy

Suppose a visible pattern moves while retaining the same grayscale values. This is the brightness-constancy assumption: follow a point on the pattern, and its image value stays the same. For example, a dot pixel with value 0.8 at its old position has value 0.8 at its new position. In the following equations we call horizontal velocity u and vertical velocity v, so the full velocity vector is \(\mathbf v=(u,v)\). Over an interval \(\Delta t\), a point moves from \((x,y)\) to \((x+u\Delta t,y+v\Delta t)\). Brightness constancy is written as

\[I(x+u\Delta t,y+v\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=I(x,y,t).\]
Symbols and meaning. I is scalar image intensity as a function of horizontal position x, vertical position y, and time t. u and v are horizontal and vertical velocities; multiplying by the small interval \(\Delta t\) converts them to displacements. The equality assumes that a moving pattern retains its brightness over that interval. This image-formation assumption defines the brightness-constancy model.

A first-order Taylor expansion gives \(I+I_xu\Delta t+I_yv\Delta t+I_t\Delta t\approx I\). Subtract the original intensity and divide by \(\Delta t\):

\[I_xu+I_yv+I_t=0,\qquad \nabla I^\mathsf T\mathbf v=-I_t.\]
Symbols and meaning. \(I_x,I_y,I_t\) denote derivatives of intensity with respect to x, y, and time. \(\nabla I=(I_x,I_y)\) is the spatial gradient and \(\mathbf v=(u,v)\) the velocity vector. A dot product sums the horizontal and vertical contributions. The scalar constraint leaves one degree of freedom in the two-component velocity.

This brightness-constancy constraint relates a temporal brightness change to spatial gradients and velocity. One scalar equation contains two unknown velocity components, leaving a family of possible motions. The linearization assumes small movements and differentiable intensity. [[horn1981]] [[lucas1981]]

The aperture problem: motion ambiguity along an edge

Consider an isolated straight edge. Its intensity changes across the edge, but hardly at all along it. The gradient points perpendicular to the edge. Brightness constancy therefore constrains the normal component of motion while leaving the tangential component ambiguous. Sliding the edge along itself changes little inside a small viewing aperture. A corner is different: two nonparallel gradients supply independent constraints. This ambiguity arises in the local visual evidence. MT physiology has motivated models in which motion estimates evolve as contextual information becomes available. [[pack2001]]

Lucas–Kanade: combine local constraints

Assume nearby pixels share one velocity. For a neighborhood \(\Omega\), minimize weighted squared residuals:

\[E(\mathbf v)=\sum_{p\in\Omega}w_p\left(\nabla I_p^\mathsf T\mathbf v+I_{t,p}\right)^2.\]
Symbols and meaning. \(E(\mathbf v)\) is the fitting error for one assumed local velocity, \(\Omega\) the neighborhood, p a pixel, and \(w_p\) a nonnegative pixel weight. The transposed gradient dotted with velocity predicts an intensity change; adding the observed temporal derivative gives its residual. Square each residual, weight it, and sum. E denotes the optimization objective.

Differentiating with respect to \(\mathbf v\) and setting the derivative to zero gives

\[\underbrace{\sum_p w_p\nabla I_p\nabla I_p^\mathsf T}_{G}\mathbf v=-\underbrace{\sum_p w_p\nabla I_p I_{t,p}}_{\mathbf b},\qquad \mathbf v=-G^{-1}\mathbf b.\]
Symbols and meaning. G is the 2 × 2 sum of weighted gradient outer products, and \(\mathbf b\) is the weighted sum of gradients multiplied by temporal derivatives. The superscript −1 means matrix inverse. Solving this linear system estimates the two velocity components. This G belongs only to the Lucas–Kanade tutorial; it never denotes the green channel or grayscale image.

An invertible G gives a unique solution; a singular G leaves ambiguity along one or more directions. The 2 × 2 matrix \(G\) summarizes local gradient directions. Two substantial eigenvalues indicate constraints in two directions. One small eigenvalue signals edge ambiguity; two small eigenvalues signal a nearly textureless patch. The spatial distribution of image gradients therefore determines how well a common local velocity can be estimated. Iterative registration can repeatedly update an alignment instead of relying on a single large linearized step. [[lucas1981]]

Horn–Schunck: add a smoothness assumption

A complementary strategy estimates a dense field while penalizing rapid spatial variation:

\[E[u,v]=\int\!\left[\left(I_xu+I_yv+I_t\right)^2+\lambda\left(\|\nabla u\|^2+\|\nabla v\|^2\right)\right]\,dx\,dy.\]
Symbols and meaning. \(E[u,v]\) scores entire horizontal and vertical velocity fields. The integral sums over image position. \(\nabla u\) and \(\nabla v\) are spatial derivatives of those fields; their squared lengths penalize rapid spatial changes. The positive constant \(\lambda\) controls how much smoothness is favored relative to brightness agreement. Both terms are inside the spatial integral.

The first term rewards agreement with image changes; the second shares information across space. The parameter \(\lambda\) controls their trade-off. Smoothness can resolve locally ambiguous regions by borrowing constraints from neighbors, but can also smear a true motion boundary. Regularization supplies the smoothness assumption used to select among candidate solutions. [[horn1981]]

Beyond the small-motion, constant-brightness approximation

Robust penalties reduce the influence of outliers such as occluded pixels. Coarse-to-fine estimation handles larger displacements by first aligning a reduced-resolution image, then refining at finer scales. Warping re-expresses one image in another's coordinates before estimating a residual. Gradient constancy supplements brightness constancy when intensity changes. These techniques address different failures and can introduce their own errors—for example, a small moving object may disappear at a coarse scale. [[black1996]] [[brox2004]] [[sun2014]]

In the following local polynomial, x is a two-dimensional pixel coordinate, A is a symmetric 2 × 2 curvature matrix, b is a two-entry linear coefficient vector, and c is a constant intensity offset. Subscripts 1 and 2 distinguish earlier and later local approximations. Farnebäck's method instead approximates local image neighborhoods with polynomials. For a quadratic \(f_1(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^\mathsf TA\mathbf x+\mathbf b_1^\mathsf T\mathbf x+c_1\), translating the pattern by \(\mathbf d\) changes the linear term to \(\mathbf b_2=\mathbf b_1-2A\mathbf d\). Solving \(2A\mathbf d=\mathbf b_1-\mathbf b_2\) connects local polynomial coefficients to displacement. A singular or noisy \(A\) again makes additional neighborhood information valuable. [[farneback2003]]

Learned methods can replace handcrafted features and update rules while retaining recognizable operations. FlowNet 2.0 learns flow estimation through stacked networks; PWC-Net combines feature pyramids, warping, and a cost volume; RAFT constructs all-pairs correlations and iteratively updates a flow field. [[ilg2017]] [[sun2018]] [[teed2020]]

Correlation: ask which shift makes two local images agree

Our model uses a finite set of candidate displacements. For an earlier patch \(A\) and a current patch \(B\), imagine shifting the second relative to the first and measuring overlap. After centering and windowing the patches, normalized correlation is

\[C(\mathbf d)=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf x}\widetilde A(\mathbf x)\widetilde B(\mathbf x+\mathbf d)}{\max\!\left(\sqrt{\sum_{\mathbf x}\widetilde A(\mathbf x)^2\sum_{\mathbf x}\widetilde B(\mathbf x)^2},10^{-8}\right)}.\]
Symbols and meaning. \(\widetilde A\) and \(\widetilde B\) are the two mean-centered, windowed image patches; the tilde labels that preprocessing. \(\mathbf x\) indexes a patch pixel and \(\mathbf d\) a candidate shift. The numerator adds products of aligned pixels; the denominator normalizes by the two patch energies, with a \(10^{-8}\) floor. This scores how well a proposed shift matches the patches.

A peak means that a displacement explains shared local structure. Several peaks mean that several candidate correspondences are plausible. A flat response can mean little usable texture; periodic structure can instead produce strong but ambiguous peaks. Normalization reduces dependence on multiplicative intensity scale. Occlusion and appearance changes alter the correspondence evidence itself. In contrast to an argmax, retaining all 81 responses preserves this ambiguity for later computation.

Why discuss motion energy in a correspondence model?

The laboratory's readout also reports entropy: a summary of how broadly the 81 normalized displacement weights are spread. Near-zero entropy means almost all weight lies on one alternative; the maximum, about 4.39, occurs when all 81 weights are equal. The unit “nats” means the calculation uses the natural logarithm. Repeated texture can give an incorrect displacement a sharp peak.

A translating pattern forms an oriented structure in space–time. Motion-energy models filter this structure with quadrature pairs, then square and sum the responses. Schematically, for filters \(f_1,f_2\) tuned to a space–time orientation,

\[E_+=(I*f_1)^2+(I*f_2)^2,\qquad M=E_+-E_-,\]
Symbols and meaning. I is an image sequence and * here is filtering over space and time. \(f_1,f_2\) are a phase-offset filter pair tuned to one motion direction. Squaring and summing their responses gives \(E_+\); another pair tuned oppositely gives \(E_-\). M subtracts those energies to express directional opposition. The + and − labels identify the two motion directions.

where the second energy channel favors the opposite direction. The quadrature sum reduces dependence on the phase of the pattern. This supplies a route to direction selectivity without explicitly tracking named dots. Motion-energy models and the correlation bank both represent relationships across space and time through populations of tuned responses. [[adelson1985]] [[watson1985]]

Reichardt-style detectors provide another instructive comparison: spatially separated inputs are temporally filtered, multiplied, and compared across opposite orderings. A schematic detector is \(R=A(t-\delta)B(t)-B(t-\delta)A(t)\). Here A(t) and B(t) are signals at two spatially separated receptors at time t, and \(\delta\) is a temporal delay; R is the difference of the two ordered products. Reversing motion reverses the temporal ordering and can reverse the response. These insect-inspired computations motivate sensitivity to spatiotemporal relationships. Our implementation evaluates local correspondence through FFT correlation. [[reichardt1961]]

From a population to a vector—and what is lost

If candidate displacement probabilities are \(p_k\), the expected displacement is \(\widehat{\mathbf d}=\sum_kp_k\mathbf d_k\). Two equal peaks at \((-3,0)\) and \((3,0)\) represent opposing motions whose weighted mean is zero. The model therefore retains the full population, its changes over time, and its expectation. This is the reason for a relatively wide motion representation alongside a compact flow field. Here, softmax provides normalized displacement weights. Probabilistic population-code theories describe how specified likelihoods and response statistics support Bayesian inference. [[simoncelli1998]] [[ma2006]]

A local, finite-range, multiframe correlation population with a small learned refinement and a pixel-prediction objective. It provides local correspondence populations for the shared recurrent observer.
