Did the cued dots change direction?

Look for the thin white ring on the left or right at the very start. It marks the patch to watch. The small dark cross in the middle is the fixation mark, not a direction cue. Keep looking at the cross while attending to the ring-marked side.

The ring disappears before two groups of pale moving dots appear. Watch the overall motion direction of the cued group. Answer Yes if that group's direction changes; answer No if only the other group changes, or neither does. A change, when present, is a ±90° turn. Individual dots can disappear and reappear; that alone is not the change.

Timing and viewing notes

These are ten new trials of the same existing ±90° task, not ten different task definitions. Each native 100×100 frame is retained in order. At 10 fps: the ring lasts 0.2 s, followed by 0.5 s of fixation alone, one 0.1 s dot-reference frame, the original baseline motion, 0.8 s after the possible change, and one 0.1 s fixation-only report frame. The final report view then stays still while you answer. There is no extra pre-roll. The ring is absent during the dots; there are no arrows, corner glyphs, or answer overlays in these stimuli.

Playback is ten times slower than the renderer's nominal 100 Hz clock. Slowing every frame also changes perceived motion speed and cue duration. This is a viewing/self-test demonstration, not formal psychophysics, a calibrated display, or a model comparison. Browser scheduling can introduce timing jitter; the MP4 files have verified 10 fps timestamps. Keep this tab visible during playback.

Default PNG playback preserves the exact rounded 8-bit native pixels, enlarged with nearest-neighbor sampling (6× on desktop, 3× on small screens). The original float arrays are retained in the archive. MP4 uses H.264 compression and is slightly lossy; use PNG mode for the authoritative pixel sequence. No interpolation or altered stimulus timing within a trial was used.
